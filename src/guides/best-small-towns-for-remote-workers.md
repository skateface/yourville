A few years ago, "remote work town" meant a college kid's parents' basement. Now it means something specific: gigabit internet run down Main Street on purpose, a coworking space in a building that used to sell hardware, and a coffee shop that has clearly seen a Zoom call or twelve. These towns didn't get remote-friendly by accident — most of them chased it, because they'd watched their downtowns hollow out and figured a laptop class was cheaper to attract than a factory.

What they have in common isn't luck, it's infrastructure and intention: broadband investment, a critical mass of people working the same way you do, and — this part matters more than people admit — something worth doing when you shut the laptop. A remote work town without a trail, a river, or a decent bar scene is just a bedroom community with better wifi.

## What to look for

- **Real broadband, not "advertised" broadband.** Ask on local subreddits or Facebook groups before you trust a coverage map.
- **A coworking space that's more than a card table.** It's a decent proxy for how many other remote workers already live there.
- **A short list of "third places."** Coffee shops, breweries, libraries — somewhere to work that isn't your kitchen table, every single day.
- **An airport or a highway.** You'll still travel. Figure out how bad that logistics story gets.

## Towns worth a look

<ul class="town-list">
  <li><strong>Bozeman, Montana</strong> — Mountains on every horizon, a university keeping the town young, and enough tech transplants that "what do you do for work" rarely gets a confused look anymore.</li>
  <li><strong>Chattanooga, Tennessee</strong> — One of the first American cities to roll out municipal gigabit fiber, which is exactly the kind of unglamorous decision that ages well. Also: a genuinely fun riverside downtown.</li>
  <li><strong>Bend, Oregon</strong> — High desert light, a river running through downtown, and a trail system that makes "quick break" mean something more interesting than a walk around the block.</li>
  <li><strong>Fayetteville, Arkansas</strong> — Cheaper than people expect, buoyed by a university and a growing bike-trail network that connects the whole region.</li>
  <li><strong>Wilmington, North Carolina</strong> — Coastal without the coastal price tag, with a film-industry history that left behind a surprisingly deep creative-professional community.</li>
  <li><strong>Traverse City, Michigan</strong> — Summers built around a bay, winters built around each other. A tight-knit remote and small-business crowd that treats the off-season like a feature, not a bug.</li>
</ul>
