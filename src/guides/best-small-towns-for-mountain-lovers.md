Elevation changes a town in ways that are easy to underestimate from a postcard. The light gets sharper. Winters get longer and more serious. The whole local economy tends to organize itself around whatever the mountains offer that season — skiing, climbing, hiking, or just the simple fact of a view that never quite gets old. Mountain towns are rarely built for convenience. They're built because the terrain was worth the inconvenience.

That trade-off is exactly what draws people in. Trailheads at the edge of downtown instead of a drive away. A cell signal that comes and goes, which stops feeling like a problem and starts feeling like a feature. A sense that the town is a base camp for something bigger than itself, rather than the whole point.

## What to look for

- **Year-round access**, not just summer. Ask what the roads and the town both look like in a hard winter.
- **A real economy beyond tourism.** Towns that only exist for ski season can feel hollow the other nine months.
- **Altitude, seriously.** Elevation affects sleep, exercise, and how quickly a beer hits — worth a visit before you commit.
- **Fire and snow risk.** Both are part of the deal in most mountain towns; know the local history before you buy in.

## Towns worth a look

<ul class="town-list">
  <li><strong>Crested Butte, Colorado</strong> — Wildflower capital in summer, serious ski town in winter, and small enough that everyone still recognizes everyone at the one grocery store.</li>
  <li><strong>Jackson, Wyoming</strong> — Grand Teton views from downtown, a genuine ranching history, and wildlife that occasionally wanders onto Main Street.</li>
  <li><strong>Telluride, Colorado</strong> — A box canyon town so dramatic it's hard to photograph badly, with a free gondola that doubles as actual daily transportation.</li>
  <li><strong>Boone, North Carolina</strong> — Blue Ridge elevation without the full Rockies commitment, anchored by a university that keeps the town lively year-round.</li>
</ul>
