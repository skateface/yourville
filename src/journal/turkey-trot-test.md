title: The Turkey Trot Test
description: Whether a small town still runs its own Thanksgiving morning fun run, and how it feels on the ground, is a good read on how much homegrown civic life is left.
date: 2026-11-24
---

A lot of small towns have some version of a Thanksgiving morning turkey trot — a fun run or walk, usually a few miles, usually timed so everyone's back home with hours to spare before dinner. It's easy to file under "cute local tradition" and move on. It's also, if you pay attention to how it actually runs, a decent read on how much homegrown civic life a town still has left.

The good version has a specific texture. People start showing up before the start line is even fully set up, in costumes that range from a store-bought turkey hat to something a kid clearly spent real effort on. The route usually runs straight down Main Street or around the square, past the same shops everyone already knows, with a few people standing on their porches just to watch. A local diner or bakery is handing out something hot at the finish. Somebody's grandfather has run it every year since before most of the current entrants were born, and everyone treats that as common knowledge rather than trivia. None of it is organized by an outside events company — it's run by the same volunteers who staff the county fair booth or the Friday night concession stand, because in a town like that, it's usually the same forty or fifty people doing all of it.

The thinner version is worth noticing too, and it's not always obvious until you're standing at the start line. Sometimes the trot still exists on paper but has been handed off to a regional race-management company, and it shows: a generic finisher's shirt, a route that avoids downtown entirely in favor of an empty business park, no one at the finish who actually knows your name. Sometimes it just quietly stopped happening a few years back and nobody organized a replacement, which tends to travel with the same slow civic thinning you'd notice if [the local paper folded](/journal/local-newspaper-test/) or [the hardware store closed](/journal/hardware-store-test/).

It matters more in some places than others. A [Family Hometown](/guides/best-small-towns-for-families/) without one is a small red flag, since that's exactly the kind of low-stakes, everyone's-invited ritual those towns tend to be built around. A [Remote Work Haven](/guides/best-small-towns-for-remote-workers/) can get away without it — plenty of people there moved for the trailhead access and the coffee, not the parade calendar — but if one exists and still feels homegrown, it's a nice sign the town hasn't lost its small-town muscle memory just because it's grown a coworking space.

You won't find this on a listing page, but you will find it on the town's own Facebook event page or its visitor-bureau calendar, usually with a few years of comment history attached. Worth a look before you assume a quiet Thanksgiving morning is just what living somewhere smaller means — sometimes it's the town, and sometimes it's just that particular town.
