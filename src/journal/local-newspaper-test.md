title: What a Town's Local Newspaper (or the Lack of One) Tells You
description: Whether a small town still has a real local newsroom — and what it covers — is one of the clearest signals of civic health you can check from a couch.
date: 2026-09-21
---

Most small-town research starts with the same handful of sources: a real estate listing, a subreddit if one exists, maybe [the local Facebook group](/journal/what-a-towns-facebook-group-tells-you/). Fewer people think to check whether the town still has an actual newspaper — and fewer still notice when the answer is no.

A lot of small towns quietly lost theirs over the last couple of decades. The building's often still there, sometimes with the masthead still painted on the window, but there's no reporter behind it anymore — just a handful of syndicated stories and a police blotter pulled from somewhere else. It's easy to miss because the town doesn't look any different from the street. What's missing is upstream of anything you can see on a drive-through: nobody's regularly showing up to council meetings, writing down what the school board actually voted on, or following a zoning fight past its first headline.

A paper that's still real has a different texture, and it's checkable in five minutes online. Are the bylines local, or is most of the content wire copy with the town's name swapped in? Does it cover things that only matter here — a contested school board seat, a Main Street storefront changing hands, a Little League team that made it to regionals — or just the same regional stories every outlet within a hundred miles is running? Is there a comments section or a letters page with actual back-and-forth, the kind of low-stakes local argument that means people are reading closely enough to disagree?

This tracks with a few of the archetypes more than others. A [Family Hometown](/guides/best-small-towns-for-families/) without a functioning local paper is missing one of the main ways residents hold a school board or a town council accountable between elections — the town may still be a great one, but you should show up expecting to do more of that watching yourself. A [Retirement Retreat](/guides/best-small-towns-for-retirees/), by contrast, often keeps a surprisingly healthy paper going longer than you'd expect, because it still has the print subscribers to support one.

None of this replaces an actual visit, or [the quiz](/quiz/) if you're still narrowing down which kind of town you're even looking for. But a live local newsroom is a decent proxy for a town that's still paying attention to itself — and a dead one is worth asking follow-up questions about, not just noting and moving on.
