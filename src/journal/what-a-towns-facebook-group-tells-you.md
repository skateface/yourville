title: What a Town's Local Facebook Group Tells You

Before the first drive-through visit, before the realtor calls back, most people researching a move already do one thing without thinking of it as research: they join the town's local Facebook group. It's usually the least curated source available on a place, which is exactly what makes it useful — nobody's managing it for outsiders, because until you show up and ask to join, it doesn't know you exist.

A healthy group has a particular texture. Someone posts a lost dog and three neighbors are out looking within the hour. A pothole gets reported and a town official actually replies, by name, with an update. The yard-sale and "free to a good home" posts outnumber the outrage posts. People disagree about a zoning change without it turning into a pile-on that runs for four days. None of that is measurable in the way a walk score is, but it's a more honest read of a town's day-to-day temperament than almost anything else you can check from a couch a thousand miles away.

A strained one has its own tells, and they're worth learning to spot. A feed that's mostly real estate agents posting listings dressed up as "isn't this house gorgeous" content is a group that's stopped being about the town and started being about selling it. Recurring flame wars over the same handful of topics — a proposed development, short-term rentals, a school board vote, whether golf carts belong on the main road — usually point at a real fault line, not a random flare-up. And a group where the same three accounts dominate every thread is telling you something about who feels entitled to speak for the place, which may or may not match who actually lives there.

The trick is reading for pattern, not for vibes off a single bad thread — every group has one unhinged post about deer. What matters is what keeps coming back. A town where "traffic" and "growth" dominate the archive is probably feeling the strain that shows up in our [Remote Work Haven](/guides/best-small-towns-for-remote-workers/) guide, where enough newcomers arrive that the old rhythm starts to wobble. A town where school board posts get genuinely long, detailed, engaged threads is a [Family Hometown](/guides/best-small-towns-for-families/) in the fullest sense — for better and worse, depending on which side of those threads you'd land on.

None of this replaces an actual visit, the kind we've [written about before](/journal/how-to-actually-visit-before-you-move/) — a Facebook group can't show you what a Tuesday afternoon downtown actually feels like. But it's free, it's available months before you can justify a plane ticket, and it's one of the few research steps that shows you a town arguing with itself instead of selling itself to you. Join before you visit, search a few keywords, and read the whole thing the way you'd read a room.
